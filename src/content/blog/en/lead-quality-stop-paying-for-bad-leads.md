Monday morning at a coaching class in Latur. The counsellor opens the sheet of weekend leads from the Meta campaign and starts dialling. The first number is switched off. The second belongs to someone who has never heard of the class. The third is a parent who enquired last week and is annoyed at being called again.

The ad report says the campaign did well: plenty of leads at a low cost per lead. The call sheet says something else. The business paid for every one of those rows, then paid again in the hours spent calling them.

## Where junk leads come from

Form ads on Meta and Google are built to make enquiring easy. The form opens inside the app with the name and phone number already filled in from the person's profile. One tap and it is submitted. That ease is why the lead count looks good, and why so many of those leads go nowhere.

The typical junk falls into a few types:

- Old or wrong numbers, because the profile carried a number the person no longer uses.
- Accidental taps by someone scrolling, or by a child on a parent's phone.
- Fake entries typed into website forms, with filler numbers and made-up names.
- Repeat enquiries from the same person across campaigns, each counted as a new lead.
- People far outside the area you serve, such as an enquiry from another state for a clinic in Nashik.

None of this is fraud by the ad platform. It is what happens when the only thing you ask the platform for is a submitted form.

## Three checks before a lead reaches sales

A **lead firewall** is a set of automatic checks that sit between the ad form and your sales team. Every lead passes through them in order, and only then appears on a caller's screen.

1. Phone validity. Is the number the right length and in a valid mobile series? Is it an obvious filler, like one digit repeated? Failures never reach a caller.
2. WhatsApp verification. A message goes to the number on WhatsApp as soon as the form is submitted, asking one simple question: which course, which locality, what time suits a call. A reply proves the number is live and the person remembers enquiring. It also starts the conversation before anyone dials.
3. Duplicate check. The number is matched against your existing enquiries and customers. A repeat is attached to the earlier record, so two callers do not ring the same parent and the lead is not counted twice.

The WhatsApp step should run on the official API, not a personal number or an unofficial tool. Your form should also say clearly that you will contact the person on WhatsApp. Consent and messaging rules apply here, so confirm the wording with a legal advisor.

## What the sales team sees

The caller no longer gets a raw sheet. They get a list sorted by quality.

At the top are leads who replied on WhatsApp, with the answer visible: "NEET, weekend batch" or "2 BHK, call after 6". Below them are valid numbers that have not replied yet, still worth a call. Duplicates show the history of the earlier enquiry. Failed numbers do not appear, though the owner can see how many each campaign sent.

After each call the caller marks one outcome: spoke and interested, spoke and not interested, wrong number, no answer, appointment or site visit booked. That one tap is the data everything else runs on.

## Tell the ad platform which leads were real

An ad platform finds more of whatever you tell it is a success. If success means a submitted form, it finds people who submit forms. Some are buyers. Many are just people who tap.

Both Meta and Google allow an advertiser to send back what happened to a lead after the form: verified, spoke to sales, booked. When a campaign is set to chase those later events instead of the raw form, the platform starts looking for people who resemble your real enquiries. The usual pattern is fewer leads and a better call sheet.

This works only if outcomes are recorded honestly and sent back regularly. A team that marks every lead "not interested" to clear the list teaches the platform the wrong lesson.

## Measure cost per real conversation, not cost per lead

Cost per lead is the number on the ad dashboard, and it is the wrong number to run a business on.

Take two campaigns for a real estate office in Pune. The figures are invented only to show the arithmetic.

- Campaign A spends ₹20,000 and brings 200 leads. Cost per lead: ₹100.
- Campaign B spends ₹20,000 and brings 80 leads. Cost per lead: ₹250.

On the dashboard, A wins easily. Now count real conversations: a person who answered, remembered enquiring and talked about a flat. If A produced 20 and B produced 40, A costs ₹1,000 per conversation and B costs ₹500. The cheaper campaign was the expensive one.

Once every lead carries a status and an outcome, **cost per real conversation** is available for each campaign, ad and form. Move the budget towards whatever produces conversations and bookings, whatever its cost per lead.

Yes, the count that reaches your sales team will usually be smaller. The leads removed are the ones that would have wasted a call, and valid but unverified numbers stay in a second list.
