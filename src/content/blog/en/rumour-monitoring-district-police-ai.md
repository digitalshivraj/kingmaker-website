It is the evening before a big procession in a district like Solapur or Jalgaon. A post in Marathi, typed in English letters, claims there was a clash on the route. There was none. By the time a constable shows a screenshot to the cyber cell, the same claim is on several public pages, with a different photo each time.

The cell typically has a few staff, a couple of computers and a long list of pages to check by hand. Nobody can read everything. The useful question for an SP office is not whether AI can help, but which part of the job it can be trusted with.

## What AI does well with Marathi and Hinglish posts

The strength of AI here is reading, not judging. It can go through far more public posts than any team can, in Devanagari Marathi, Marathi typed in Roman letters, Hindi and the English mix people actually write in.

Three jobs suit it:

- Reading volume. It reads public posts, comments and captions and keeps the ones that mention the district, its towns, known trouble spots or events on the bandobast calendar.
- Grouping similar posts. The same claim comes back with changed spelling, a new photo or one line added. AI puts these versions in one cluster, so the officer sees one item with many copies, not a long list.
- Flagging spikes. When posts about one place or one event rise sharply against the usual level, it raises a flag with the cluster attached.

That is the whole of it: **read, group, flag**. For a small cell, that alone changes the working day.

## What it cannot do

Three limits belong in any proposal.

It cannot decide intent. Someone sharing a rumour to warn family, someone mocking it and someone trying to provoke can use nearly the same words. Sarcasm, local slang and code words are easy for a machine to misread.

It cannot judge truth on its own. The system does not know what happened in a town in Nanded district last night. Only the police station concerned knows that. AI can show that a claim is spreading. It cannot tell you the claim is false.

It cannot replace an officer's call. Whether to ignore a post, watch it, issue a clarification, call a peace committee meeting or register an offence is a decision with legal and public order consequences. **That decision belongs to an officer**, with a name and a rank against it.

## Why every alert goes to a person first

An AI flag is a lead, not a finding. If a flag triggered action directly, one misread joke could end in a notice to a citizen who did nothing wrong, and the department would carry that mistake.

So the working order should be fixed:

1. The system raises an alert with the cluster of posts, the place or event named, and how the volume has moved.
2. A cyber cell officer opens the original posts and reads them, not just the summary.
3. The officer checks the claim with the police station concerned or the control room.
4. The officer marks the alert: false claim spreading, true event, or nothing of concern.
5. Any action, from a public clarification to a request to a platform, is approved by the authorised officer and recorded.

The record matters as much as the alert. **Who saw it, what they checked and what they decided** goes on file, so the office can answer for it later.

## A daily cyber bulletin the SP can read in five minutes

An SP does not need a wall of charts. The SP needs one page each morning, in Marathi or English, that reads in five minutes on a phone.

It carries:

- The top few clusters from the last day, each in two lines: what is being claimed and where it is spreading.
- The status of each, as marked by the cyber cell: verified false, verified true or still being checked.
- Places and events to watch today, read against the bandobast calendar.
- Action taken yesterday and anything waiting for the SP's approval.

AI can draft this bulletin from alerts the officers have already reviewed. The cyber cell in-charge reads it, corrects it and sends it. **Nothing reaches the SP's phone unreviewed.**

## Privacy limits to settle before a pilot

A monitoring system the public does not trust can hurt the department more than a rumour does. The limits should be agreed in writing before any pilot starts.

- Public posts only. No private WhatsApp groups, no private accounts, no encrypted chats.
- Topics and places, not people. The system watches claims, places and events. It does not build a file on any individual. **No profiling of a person without legal process.**
- Limited access. Only named officers log in, every search is logged, and data is kept only as long as the department's policy allows.

## Common questions

### Can the system read private WhatsApp groups?

No. It reads only posts that are public on social media platforms. A message from a closed group reaches the cyber cell only when someone forwards it to the police through the usual channels.

### Can AI tell whether a post is true or false?

No, not on its own. It can show that a claim is spreading and where, but only the police station concerned can confirm what happened on the ground. The officer marks the alert true or false after checking.
