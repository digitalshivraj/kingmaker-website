It is 9 at night in a 25-bed hospital in Latur. A patient with a fractured leg is at the door, and the receptionist is on the phone to the first-floor nurse, asking whether bed 7 in the male ward is free. The whiteboard at the nursing station says it is occupied. That patient went home at 4 in the afternoon. Nobody wiped the name.

This is how beds are tracked in many small hospitals: a whiteboard, a register and the memory of whoever is on duty. It works while one person knows everything. It breaks at shift change, on a busy night, and on the day that person is on leave.

## Where the whiteboard and memory fail

The whiteboard hangs in one place. Reception cannot see it, the owner cannot see it from home, and the night nurse inherits whatever the day nurse remembered to write.

The typical results are familiar to any nursing superintendent:

- A discharged patient's bed still shows as full, so a new patient is turned away.
- A bed that is still being cleaned is promised to an incoming patient.
- A bed held for tomorrow's planned surgery is given away during the night shift.
- A patient is shifted from the general ward to a special room, and billing never hears about it.

None of this is carelessness. The board depends on someone remembering to update it, and that someone is also looking after patients.

## A live bed board, ward by ward

A digital bed board replaces the whiteboard with **one board that looks the same on every screen**: the reception computer, the nursing station and the owner's phone.

Beds are grouped by ward, under the names the hospital already uses: general male, general female, special rooms, ICU if you have one. Each bed is a tile showing one of four statuses.

- Occupied: the patient's name, the admitting doctor and the date of admission.
- Vacant: clean and ready for the next patient.
- Cleaning: the patient has left, housekeeping has not finished.
- Reserved: held for a planned admission, such as a surgery listed for the next morning.

The receptionist at the door no longer calls upstairs. She looks at the board, sees two vacant beds in the male ward and one in cleaning, and answers the family standing in front of her.

## Admission, transfer and discharge update the board

Nobody maintains the board as a separate job. It changes because of three entries the staff already make.

1. Admission. The receptionist or nurse picks the patient, chooses a vacant bed and saves. The bed turns occupied.
2. Transfer. The nurse moves the patient to another bed. The old bed goes to cleaning, the new bed turns occupied, and the move is recorded with its date and time.
3. Discharge. When the discharge is completed, the bed goes to cleaning. When the ward staff mark it clean, it turns vacant.

The order matters. A bed cannot be given to a second patient while the first is still on it, and it does not show as vacant until someone marks it clean.

If the patient first came through OPD, the details are already on record. For a hospital that runs its OPD the way we describe in our post on [OPD tokens on WhatsApp](/blog/opd-tokens-on-whatsapp/), admission is one more step on the same patient record, not a fresh form.

## Charges that start from the admission entry

In a paper system the IPD bill is built at discharge, from the register and from memory. This is typically where money slips away: two days in a special room charged at the general ward rate, medicines issued to the ward but never added, a lab test done on the third day and forgotten.

When the board is tied to billing, **the bill opens when the bed is allotted**. Bed charges are counted from the admission entry, at the rate you have set for that ward. A transfer changes the rate from the time of the transfer. Medicines and lab tests ordered for an admitted patient are added against the same admission.

At discharge, the billing desk reviews a bill that already exists. It does not rebuild one while the family waits. ## What the owner sees each morning

Before rounds, the owner opens the phone and sees the hospital as it stands. No calls to the night staff.

- Beds occupied, vacant, in cleaning and reserved, ward by ward.
- Yesterday's admissions, transfers and discharges.
- Each admitted patient with days of stay and the running bill.
- Advances collected and amounts still due.
- Beds that have sat in cleaning since the previous day.

The nursing superintendent uses the same view differently: to decide which ward needs an extra pair of hands on the next shift, and which reserved beds must be ready before the surgeon arrives.

## Moving from the whiteboard to the screen

The change is done in a set order, so the ward never runs without a board.

1. List every ward and bed, with the hospital's own bed numbers and daily rates.
2. Create logins for the owner, nurses, reception and billing, each with a role.
3. On a chosen day, enter the patients who are currently admitted.
4. Run the whiteboard alongside the screen until the staff trust the screen. Then take the whiteboard down.

## Common questions

### Does every nurse need a computer to use the bed board?

No. The board opens on a phone, so a nurse can change a bed's status from the ward itself. A computer at the nursing station and at reception helps, but the hospital can start with the devices it already has.

### Who is allowed to change a bed's status?

The owner decides, through staff roles. A typical arrangement lets nurses admit, transfer and mark beds clean, lets reception view the board, and keeps rates and bill edits with the billing desk and the owner. This kind of [access control](/services/security-compliance/) also means patient names are seen only by staff with a login.
