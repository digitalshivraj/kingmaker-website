Open the Facebook page of a typical corporator in Nashik or Solapur and you will see a pattern. Diwali greetings, a Shiv Jayanti banner, birthday wishes for a senior leader, twelve photos from a bhoomipujan. Then nothing for three weeks.

A resident who wants to know when the drain work will finish, or how to apply for a scheme, finds no answer there. The page tells people the leader exists. It does not tell them what the office does.

## Why festival and event posts are not enough

Every leader posts the same greeting on the same morning. A citizen scrolling past ten similar banners does not remember whose was whose. Keep the greetings, they are polite, but they carry no information about your work.

Event photos have the same limit. A stage, a garland and a ribbon show that something happened. They do not say which road, how much is complete, or who benefits.

The bigger cost is the silence between occasions. **A page that speaks only on festivals looks like an office that opens only on festivals.** A weekly plan fixes this by giving the team something specific to post on fixed days.

## A weekly rhythm with four kinds of posts

Four posts a week are enough for a ward or constituency office. Each has a different job.

1. Monday, work done. One completed or ongoing work, with a clear photo, the ward or area, and the status in one line. For example: "Pipeline work in Ward 12, laying complete, road restoration starts next."
2. Wednesday, citizen issue solved. One complaint the office closed: what the problem was, which department acted, what changed. Take the citizen's permission before showing a face or name, and never post phone numbers or documents.
3. Friday, event. One event from the week, with two or three good photos instead of twenty, and one line on why it matters to residents.
4. Sunday, explainer. One useful thing: how to apply for a scheme, which papers to carry to a camp, where to report a broken streetlight. Confirm details with the department before posting, because rules and dates change.

Festival greetings continue on top of this. They are extra, not the plan.

The first two posts need raw material. If the office keeps a list of sanctioned and completed works, Monday's post is easy, and a [public works tracker for the MLA fund](/blog/mla-development-works-tracker/) shows citizens the full picture behind it. Wednesday's post needs a record of closed complaints, which is hard to produce when [complaints are lost in WhatsApp groups](/blog/mla-office-grievance-system/).

## Short Marathi videos shot on a phone

A video of the leader standing at the site, speaking plainly in Marathi, does more than a designed banner. People want to hear the voice and see the place.

You do not need a camera crew. You need a phone and a few habits:

- Hold the phone upright, because most people will watch on a phone.
- Stand at the actual location: the new water tank, the repaired school wall, the camp counter.
- Make one point per video: the problem, what was done, what comes next.
- Speak the way you speak in the ward, not in press-note Marathi.
- Add Marathi captions, because people often watch with the sound off.

Keep each video short enough that someone at a tea stall watches to the end. If you have three points, make three videos.

## Answer comments and messages like an office counter

A comment under a post is a citizen standing at your counter. If nobody answers, they notice, and so does everyone else reading.

Give one person in the office the job of checking comments and inbox messages every working day. Three rules help.

- Reply to genuine questions in Marathi, with a specific answer or a specific next step.
- When a comment is really a complaint, tell the citizen it has been noted and move it to the office register. Do not ask for personal details in public comments.
- Do not argue with abuse. State the facts once and leave the rest.

## Track what people respond to

At the end of each month, sit with the team and look at what happened. You do not need a paid tool. The platforms' own insights and a simple sheet will do.

For each post, note:

- its type: work, issue solved, event or explainer
- shares and saves
- comments that asked a real question
- messages or office visits that mentioned the post

Typically, explainers and solved issues get passed around in locality groups, while greeting banners collect likes and little else. Your area may differ, which is why you check.

Then adjust: more of what people share and ask about, less of what gets silent likes. **Count questions and shares, not likes.** If you pay to promote anything, put the money behind posts that already did well on their own.

## Common questions

### Which platform should a local leader's office start with?

Start with the platform where residents of your area already comment and message you. For many offices in Maharashtra that is typically Facebook, with Instagram for younger voters. Do one platform properly before adding a second.

### Who should run the page, the leader or the team?

The team should run it, with the leader appearing in videos and approving anything sensitive. Name one person who owns the weekly plan and one who answers comments, so neither job depends on who happens to be free.

### Do the posts need to change during an election period?

Yes. Once the model code of conduct applies, there are rules on political advertising, content and spending on social media. Confirm the current requirements with Election Commission guidance and your party's legal advisor before posting or promoting anything.
