A physician in Latur finishes a consultation with a patient who has had diabetes for some years. She adjusts the dose, asks for a sugar test and says, "Come back after three months." The patient nods, folds the prescription into his shirt pocket and leaves.

Three months later, nobody remembers which week that was. The tablets finish, the medical shop gives the same strip again, and the review happens only when something goes wrong. The doctor never learns that the patient was due.

## Patients forget the date, not the advice

Ask a patient who missed a review and you will typically hear the same thing. They remember what the doctor said about food, walking and tablets. What they lost was the date.

"After three months" is not a date. It is an instruction the patient has to convert into a day, carry through a sowing season, a family wedding and school exams, and then act on by calling the clinic. Each step is a place to drop out.

The clinic has the same gap, whether it is in Latur, Jalgaon or Solapur. The paper register shows who came today. It does not show who was supposed to come and did not. So **the problem is the date, not the patient's attitude**, and a date is something a system can hold.

## Set the follow-up date before the patient leaves the room

The fix begins at the end of the consultation. When the doctor says "three months", that should become **an actual date in the patient's record**, entered while the patient is still in the chair.

It should be one tap, not a form. A few choices are enough:

- A standard gap: one week, two weeks, one month, three months, six months.
- A specific date picked from a calendar.
- A short note for the patient, such as "bring fasting sugar report".

The note earns its place. A patient who arrives without the test the doctor asked for has to come again.

Before saving, the doctor or receptionist confirms the mobile number and asks whether WhatsApp reminders are fine. For an elderly patient the number often belongs to a son or daughter. Record that, because that is the person who will read the message.

If the clinic already writes prescriptions in software, such as our [hospital management system](/products/hospital-management-system/), the follow-up date belongs next to the prescription.

## A WhatsApp reminder that carries a booking link

A few days before the due date, the patient gets a WhatsApp message in Marathi or English. It says the review is due, repeats the doctor's note about reports, and carries a link.

**The link does the work.** A message that says "please call the clinic" hands the patient one more task, and the clinic phone is usually busy during OPD hours. A link opens the doctor's slots, the patient picks one, and the booking is done. We have written about [why a booking link works better than a phone call](/blog/patient-booking-link-for-doctors/), and the same reasoning applies to reviews. On the day, the patient can [take an OPD token on WhatsApp](/blog/opd-tokens-on-whatsapp/) and wait at home instead of in the hall.

Keep the message plain: patient's name, doctor's name, due date, the note, the link. Leave the diagnosis out, because many families share a phone. Our post on [patient data and the DPDP Act](/blog/clinic-patient-data-dpdp-act/) explains what a clinic should change. For anything specific to your practice, confirm with a legal advisor.

Send it the official way. Reminders from a staff member's personal number, or from an unofficial bulk tool, put that number at risk of being blocked. Use official [WhatsApp automation](/services/whatsapp-automation/), with an approved message template and patients who have agreed to receive it.

## A second reminder only for those who did not book

Some patients read the first message and mean to book later. A second message, closer to the date, is for them.

The rule is simple: the second reminder goes **only to patients who have not booked**. Someone already holding a slot should not be asked again. This works only when reminders and bookings sit in one system, so it knows who has booked.

Stop at two. A third and fourth message turn a helpful clinic into a nuisance, and patients begin ignoring everything the clinic sends, including lab reports and queue updates.

## The missed follow-up list your staff can call

After two messages, some patients will still not have booked. Some do not use WhatsApp, some have changed numbers, some have moved to another doctor. A message cannot tell you which.

Here a person takes over. The system gives the clinic **a list of missed follow-ups**: patients whose due date has passed without a booking, each with name, phone number, due date and the doctor's note.

A routine for the front desk:

1. Open the list on a fixed day each week, in a quiet hour after OPD.
2. Call each patient, or the family member whose number is saved.
3. Book the slot during the call if the patient agrees.
4. Mark the outcome: booked, will come later, moved to another doctor, wrong number.

Who gets called first is the doctor's judgement. In a chronic-care practice, a patient whose medicines were just changed may need the call before someone due for a routine check. The list does not decide that. It only makes sure nobody is invisible.

## Common questions

### Will patients find the reminders irritating?

Usually not, if the clinic asks permission at the desk and stops at two messages. A reminder for a review the doctor advised is a service, not an advertisement. Never mix offers or promotions into it.

### What about patients who do not use WhatsApp?

They are handled by the call list. Save a family member's number at registration where possible. If no booking appears by the due date, the patient shows up on the missed list and the staff call.

### Should the reminder mention the illness?

No. The message should carry the patient's name, the doctor's name, the due date and the booking link, and nothing about the condition. Data protection rules apply to clinics as well, so confirm the details with a legal advisor.
