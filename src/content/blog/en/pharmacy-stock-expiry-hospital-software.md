Picture the end of the month at a small hospital in Latur. The pharmacy manager pulls a carton from the back shelf and finds strips of an antibiotic that are past their expiry date. Nobody knew they were there. The distributor will not take them back now.

The same week, a patient is discharged and the bill misses the injections given on the second night. The nurse took them from the pharmacy on a chit, and the chit never reached the billing desk. Neither loss appears in any register. The owner only feels it when the distributor's payment falls due and the cash is short.

## Where the money leaks

In a typical small hospital pharmacy, money goes missing in three quiet ways.

- Expired stock: medicines bought in bulk for a scheme discount, pushed behind newer cartons, and found after the date.
- Unbilled medicines: items issued to the ward on a chit, given in an emergency, or handed over with a promise to pay later.
- Stock nobody counted: the register says 40 strips, the shelf has 31, and no one can say whether the gap is a wrong purchase entry, a sale without a bill, or something worse.

None of these is dramatic. Each is a small amount on its own. Together they are **money the hospital has already paid out** and will not see again.

## Record batch and expiry when stock comes in

The fix starts at purchase entry. When the distributor's invoice arrives with the cartons, each line is entered with its batch number, expiry date, quantity, purchase rate and MRP.

From then on, the software does not just know that you hold 60 strips of a tablet. It knows which batches they belong to and when each one expires. At the counter it offers the **earliest-expiring batch first**, so old stock moves before new stock.

Purchase entry also gives you a check on the distributor. What was invoiced, what was received and what came free under a scheme are all on record in one place.

## Sell against the prescription so every medicine is billed

When pharmacy and OPD run on the same system, the doctor's prescription appears at the pharmacy counter. The pharmacist opens it, picks the items, and the bill is made. Stock drops at the same moment. Nobody retypes anything and nobody has to decode handwriting.

For admitted patients, medicines are issued against the patient's name and added to the running bill at the time of issue. The discharge bill already includes the injections from the second night. The chit disappears.

The rule to enforce is simple: **nothing leaves the shelf without a bill or an issue entry**. Walk-in customers are billed as counter sales in the same way.

One caution. The sale of prescription medicines, pharmacy records and GST on medicines are all governed by rules. Confirm what you must record and retain with your CA and the drug licensing authority.

## Near-expiry alerts and returns to the distributor

Once every batch has a date, the software can list what will expire well before it does. The pharmacy manager chooses how far ahead to look. Each item on that list has four options, roughly in this order:

1. Move it to the front of the shelf and sell it first.
2. Tell the doctors which brands are in stock, so that prescriptions match the shelf where that is medically appropriate.
3. Return it to the distributor while their return terms still allow it.
4. Reduce or skip that item in the next order.

Return terms differ from one distributor and one company to another, so ask each of yours for their **policy in writing**. When a return is made, enter it against the same batch and record the credit note when it arrives. Otherwise returned stock becomes one more thing nobody can account for.

## The daily report the owner reads on a phone

An owner doing rounds in Kolhapur or Jalgaon is not sitting at the pharmacy counter. What they need is one report at the end of the day, on a phone, that answers these questions:

- What was sold today, split by OPD, IPD and counter sales, and by cash, UPI and credit?
- What purchases were entered, and from which distributor?
- What is the value of stock on the shelf?
- Which items are below their reorder level?
- Which batches are nearing expiry?
- Did anyone change a stock figure by hand, and who?

The last question matters most. With staff roles, the pharmacist can sell but only the owner or the manager can adjust stock. Pair that with a **small physical count of a few items every day**, checked against the software, and gaps show up while they can still be explained.

## Common questions

### Do we have to count the full stock before starting?

Yes, the opening stock has to be counted once, with batch and expiry for every item. It comes first, because every report after it depends on that count. It is common to find expired and forgotten stock during this count itself.

### Does the pharmacy need a new computer?

No. The system runs on phones and on the computers the hospital already has. The pharmacist can bill from the existing counter computer, and the owner can read the report on a phone.

### Who should be allowed to change stock figures?

Only the owner, or one person the owner names. Everyone else should be able to sell, issue and enter purchases, but not edit quantities. Every manual adjustment should carry a name and a reason, so that it shows in the daily report.
