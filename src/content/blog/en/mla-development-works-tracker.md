An MLA office in Latur district gets a cement road, a community hall and a set of solar street lights sanctioned in one year. The works are completed. Then a voter from another part of the constituency says, "Nothing has happened here."

He is not lying. He has never seen the list. The board at the site has faded, the inauguration photo is buried in a WhatsApp group, and the only full record is a file in the office and a spreadsheet on one PA's laptop.

## Why citizens do not see the work

Development work is local by nature. A person notices the road in front of their house and little else. A drain in the next ward, a classroom across the taluka, a water tank in another village: none of it reaches them unless someone shows it.

Offices try. They post photos on social media, print a booklet, put up flex boards. Each of these shows a few works to a few people for a short while. None answers the question a citizen actually asks: **what was done in my village or ward**, and what is still pending?

A corporator in Nashik or Nagpur faces the same question at ward level.

## What the public list should show

The answer is a public page, in Marathi and English, where anyone can pick their village or ward and see every work there. No login and no download for the viewer.

Each entry needs only a few things:

- **Name and place of the work**, written the way local people describe it.
- Type: road, water, school, lighting, hall or drainage.
- Fund and year, for example the MLA local development fund or another scheme.
- Sanctioned amount in ₹, taken from the sanction papers.
- Status: proposed, sanctioned, work order issued, in progress or completed.
- Photos before, during and after, each with a date.
- The implementing department or agency.

Show pending and delayed works too. A list with only completed works reads like an advertisement. A list that says "work order issued, work not started" reads like a record, and people trust a record.

## Updates from the field, on a phone

The list goes stale if only one person in the office can update it. The people who actually see the works are karyakartas and the PA on tour. They carry phones, not laptops.

A workable routine looks like this:

1. Office staff create the work entry from the sanction papers: name, place, amount and fund.
2. A field worker assigned to that area opens the work on a phone, takes a photo at the site and picks the new status.
3. The phone attaches the date and location to the photo, so nobody types them.
4. The update waits in the office dashboard until a staff member checks and approves it.
5. Only then does it appear on the public page.

The app has to run on a low-cost phone and a weak network, saving the photo and sending it when the signal returns. That is how we build [field apps for basic phones](/services/mobile-apps/). ## A link people can share on WhatsApp

Every village and ward gets its own link. So does every single work.

This changes how the office responds. When someone in a WhatsApp group says nothing has been done, the karyakarta does not need to argue. They paste the link for that village. One tap opens the works, the photos and the amounts.

The same link can go on the site board as a QR code and in the office's reply to a citizen's complaint.

Works and complaints belong side by side. A citizen reporting a broken road should be able to see whether a road work is already sanctioned there. ## Keeping figures accurate and up to date

A public figure that turns out to be wrong does more damage than no figure at all. Three habits protect the office.

First, **every amount comes from a document**, not from memory. Keep a scan of the sanction paper attached to the entry in the dashboard. Label whether a number is the sanctioned amount, the work order value or the amount paid. These differ, and mixing them invites questions.

Second, show a "last updated" date on every work. An in-progress work with an old date tells the office to send someone to the site. A weekly review of works with old dates keeps the list honest.

Third, keep a history. When a status or amount changes, the system should record who changed it and when, so a correction can be explained.

Two cautions. Photos should show the work, not the faces of residents. And rules on publishing fund details, and on publicity during an election period, should be confirmed with the sanctioning office, election officials or a legal advisor before you publish.

## Common questions

### Should the cost of each work be public?

Yes, the sanctioned amount is what makes the list credible. Take it from the sanction document and label it clearly. If you are unsure whether a figure may be published, confirm with the sanctioning office first.

### Who should be allowed to update a work?

Field workers should send updates and office staff should approve them. This keeps the list current without letting a wrong photo or status go public. Each field worker should see only the villages or wards assigned to them.

### Do citizens need to install an app to see the list?

No, the public list should open from a link in any phone browser. An app helps citizens who also want to raise complaints, but a shared link must work for people who will never install anything.
