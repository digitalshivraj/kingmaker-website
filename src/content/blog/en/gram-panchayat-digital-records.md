A resident walks into a Gram Panchayat office in Latur district and asks for a copy of last year's house tax receipt. The bank wants it for a loan file. The gram sevak is away at the other Gram Panchayat in their charge, last year's receipt book is in a locked cupboard, and the clerk starts turning pages of the demand register.

Nobody here is doing anything wrong. The records exist, but on paper, in one room, and only one or two people know where to look. This post sets out which records a village office can move online first.

## Which registers take the most time today

A Gram Panchayat keeps many prescribed registers, but four kinds of work fill most of a typical day.

- Tax: the property list, the yearly demand, collections, arrears and the receipt book.
- Water: connections, bills, payments and the list of who has not paid.
- Certificates: a resident applies, the office checks its records, and the resident returns to ask if it is ready.
- Meetings: notices, attendance, minutes and resolutions of the monthly meeting and the Gram Sabha, written in the proceedings book.

The first two repeat every year, the third keeps people waiting, and the fourth is the memory of the village. **Start where the repetition is highest**, which is usually tax and water.

## Start with tax and water bill records and receipts

Tax and water records are the best first step because the data is already structured. Every property and every water connection has an owner, an amount due and an amount paid.

Do the work in this order.

1. Enter the property list and water connections from the current registers, with names in Marathi and a mobile number where the resident gives one.
2. Enter the opening arrears for each account, and have a second person check them against the paper register.
3. From then on, record every payment in the system. Each produces a **numbered receipt** that can be printed or sent on WhatsApp.
4. When the counter closes, match the collection list on screen with the cash and bank deposits.
5. Use the pending list, ward by ward, to plan recovery visits.

After this, the Latur resident's request is a search by name, and anyone in the office can reprint the receipt.

One caution. Receipt formats, tax rates and whether a digital receipt can stand in for the receipt book are set by government rules, not by a vendor. Confirm with the Panchayat Samiti or Zilla Parishad before changing how receipts are issued, and keep the paper register running alongside.

## Track certificate requests from application to issue

The certificate itself is rarely the slow part. The trouble is **not knowing where the request is**. The resident visits again and again, and each time someone hunts for the application.

Give every request a number and a status:

- Received, with the applicant's name, mobile number and type of certificate
- Documents checked, or returned with a note on what is missing
- Verified against the office record
- Signed by the authorised person
- Issued, with the date and the name of the person who collected it

The resident gets the application number on a slip or by message. Anyone in the office can answer "where is my certificate" by looking it up, and the sarpanch can see what is pending and with whom.

Which certificates a Gram Panchayat may issue, and through which channel, is laid down by the department, so confirm that list first.

## Store meeting minutes and resolutions so they can be searched

Ask any gram sevak about the resolution that approved a pipeline repair some years ago. It is in a proceedings book. Which book, and which page, is another matter.

For each meeting, store the date, type, attendance and minutes. For each resolution, store its number, subject, decision, the person responsible and whether the action is complete. Attach a photo or scan of the signed page.

Now a search for "pipeline" or "anganwadi" lists every resolution on that subject, in order. An audit query or a question in the Gram Sabha can be answered from a phone. **Treat the signed proceedings book as the official record.** The digital copy is the index that makes it usable.

## Make it work on a phone with weak network

Many village offices, in Nandurbar as much as in Sindhudurg, have one old computer and a signal that comes and goes. Ask any vendor for these things.

- Entries can be saved on the phone without network and sent to the server when the signal returns.
- Screens are light, in Marathi, with large buttons that work on a low-cost Android phone.
- Each person has a login and a role: the clerk records collections, the gram sevak approves, the sarpanch views reports.
- Data is backed up on a server in India, so a lost phone does not mean a lost register.

## Common questions

### Does the office have to stop using paper registers?

No, the prescribed registers continue until the competent authority tells you otherwise in writing. Run the digital record alongside and use it for searching, receipts and reports.

### Will this replace the government portals the office already uses?

No, it works alongside them. Wherever the state or central government requires a portal for accounts, plans or services, that portal comes first. The office system covers the daily working records around it.

### Who can see residents' data?

Only the people the office gives a login to, and only what their role allows. Ask the vendor where the data is hosted, who can access it and how you get a full copy if you stop.
