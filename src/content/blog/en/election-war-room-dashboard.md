It is the last stretch of a Vidhan Sabha campaign in Solapur district. The war room is a rented hall with six laptops, a television on a news channel and a whiteboard nobody has updated since morning. The candidate calls with one question: which booths still have no committee?

Three people open three different Excel sheets and give three different answers. The information exists, but it sits in WhatsApp groups, in a taluka president's diary and in someone's memory. Here is what one screen should show instead.

## Booth coverage and the gaps

The first view should answer the candidate's question without a phone call. Every booth in the constituency appears in one list, and each row shows:

- whether a booth head and committee are in place, with names and phone numbers
- how many households the team has contacted so far
- when someone from the campaign last visited
- whether a polling agent and a backup have been named

The screen should **show the gaps first**. Booths with no committee or no recent visit go to the top, grouped by area. A manager with hundreds of booths needs the short list that deserves a call today.

Our post on [the booth committee dashboard a Vidhan Sabha head should see daily](/blog/booth-committee-dashboard/) goes deeper into this view.

## Karyakarta activity by area

A list of registered karyakartas tells you very little. The war room needs activity: who did house visits today, who attended the corner meeting, who has stopped reporting.

Show it by area, not as a leaderboard of names. If activity drops across a ward in Nashik city or a whole rural circle, the area in-charge should hear about it the same day. **A quiet area is a warning.**

This works only if workers actually report. Reporting has to be a short form in Marathi, with a photo, on the phone they already own. Our post on [a karyakarta app that ground workers actually open](/blog/karyakarta-app-ground-workers/) covers how to get there.

## Issues and complaints from the field

During door-to-door contact, voters tell karyakartas things. A water tanker that stopped coming. A road promised long ago. A family upset about a pending certificate. In a typical campaign this is posted in a WhatsApp group and scrolls out of sight.

The control room should see each issue as a record: the booth, the category, who reported it and **one named owner** responsible for a reply. If the same water complaint arrives from many booths in one part of the constituency, the candidate should know before the next sabha there, not after.

## Events, rallies and the day's schedule

One schedule should hold the candidate's sabhas, padyatras, corner meetings and visiting leaders' programmes. Each entry should show the place, the time, the person responsible and the status of permissions.

Rallies, vehicles and loudspeakers are regulated during an election. What needs permission, and from whom, is set by election rules, so confirm it with the returning officer's office or your legal advisor. The screen's job is simpler: show which of tomorrow's events are cleared and which are pending.

After each event, the person responsible adds photos and a short note, so the record is complete.

## Polling day: turnout updates from booth agents

On polling day the screen changes. Campaign views step back and one view takes over: every booth, whether the agent is present, the latest turnout figure and any problem flagged.

The order of work:

1. Before polling day, each booth gets a named agent and a backup, with a login tied to that booth alone.
2. In the morning, each agent marks presence, and booths with no agent show up at once.
3. At fixed points in the day, a turnout figure is reported for each booth.
4. The war room compares areas and tells each area in-charge where turnout is running low.

What an agent may carry and do inside or near a polling station is governed by Election Commission rules. Confirm the reporting arrangement with the returning officer's office before you plan it. Our [election technology](/services/election-technology/) work is built to those rules.

## Access by role so data does not leak

A war room screen holds worker phone numbers, booth-wise strength and a frank list of weak areas. One screenshot in the wrong group hands all of it to the opposing camp.

So the system should decide who sees what:

- The candidate and campaign manager see the whole constituency.
- An area in-charge sees only the booths under them.
- A booth head sees one booth.
- War room operators can enter and view data, but cannot export it.
- Polling agents see only their reporting form.

Each person logs in with their own phone number. **No shared passwords.** The system keeps a log of who viewed or downloaded what, and access ends when someone leaves the campaign.

Voter and worker lists are personal data, so ask your legal advisor how data protection law applies to your campaign, and consider a [security and compliance review](/services/security-compliance/) before work starts.

## Common questions

### Does a war room need a video wall for this?

No, a laptop connected to a television is enough. The same views open on the campaign manager's phone. What matters is that everyone reads from the same data.

### Will booth-level workers manage it on basic smartphones?

Yes, if the field app is built for low-cost phones and weak networks, and works in Marathi. Keep each report to a short form and a photo. If reporting feels like paperwork, workers drift back to WhatsApp groups.

### Can a campaign keep voter and worker details in software?

Yes, but how personal data is collected, stored and used is governed by Election Commission rules and data protection law.
