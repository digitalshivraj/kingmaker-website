A purchase officer in a Zilla Parishad office in Jalgaon has a thin file on the table. The department head wants a small portal to track applications, and the budget is modest. A full paper tender feels too heavy for a job this size, but a quotation on a vendor's letterhead will invite an audit query.

Across the state, a small software company in Latur has the opposite problem. It registered on the Government e-Marketplace, uploaded its documents, and has not received a single order. Both are looking at the same portal from different sides.

## What GeM is and why departments use it

GeM, the Government e-Marketplace, is the national online portal where government offices and public bodies buy goods and services from registered sellers. Private companies, including very small ones, sell on it.

For a department, the result is **a purchase that stands up in audit**. The requirement, the sellers who were compared, the price, the order and the payment all sit on the portal.

GeM lists services as well as goods, so software work is typically bought as a service: development, hosting, maintenance or support. Whether your office must use GeM, or may choose it, depends on the purchase rules that apply to your department. Confirm that with your accounts officer.

## Three ways a department can buy

In general terms GeM has three buying modes, and they get stricter as the amount grows.

1. Direct purchase. For small orders, the buyer picks a listed seller whose offering meets the requirement and places the order on the portal.
2. L1 comparison. For mid-sized orders, the buyer compares listed sellers that meet the requirement and orders from the one with the lowest price.
3. Bid or reverse auction. For larger orders, the department publishes its requirement on GeM, eligible sellers quote, and the lowest qualifying offer gets the order.

**The value of the purchase decides which mode applies.** The limits are set by government financial rules and are revised from time to time. Check the current limits on the [GeM portal](https://gem.gov.in) and with your accounts officer.

So "without a tender" is true for a small job: no separate paper tender, no sealed envelopes. A larger job still goes to competition, but the bid runs online on GeM. Do not split one requirement into smaller orders to stay under a limit.

## What a vendor must have before the first order

A small IT vendor in Nashik, Solapur or Amravati needs four things.

- Udyam registration. This is your proof of being a micro or small enterprise. Government purchase policy gives such enterprises certain benefits.
- GST registration. The department needs a proper tax invoice. Ask your CA what applies to you.
- GeM seller registration. The portal verifies the business and its authorised person. The exact list of documents is on the portal itself.
- A catalogue or service listing. List what you sell, in the right category, with a clear scope and a price.

Before you upload anything, check that the business name, address and signatory **read the same on every document**. A mismatch between two certificates is a typical reason a registration gets stuck.

## Where a demo fits in GeM buying

Software is hard to buy from a specification sheet. An officer in Kolhapur who has never seen a grievance dashboard cannot easily write down what one should do.

A demo solves this, and it sits before the purchase, not inside it:

1. The department explains the problem to one or more vendors.
2. Each vendor shows a working demo, ideally with the department's own forms and Marathi labels.
3. The department writes its requirement in its own words.
4. The purchase goes through the GeM mode that the value calls for.

**A demo is not a commitment to buy.** It does not replace comparison or bidding where the rules require them, and the requirement should describe the work, not one vendor's product. What it does is make the scope clear, so the order, the deliverables and the payment stages are written properly.

If the software will hold citizen data, settle hosting and access questions at this stage too. Our post on [what the DPDP Act means for government software](/blog/dpdp-act-government-software/) lists what to ask.

## Why small vendor listings fail

Small vendors typically do not fail on capability. **They fail on the listing.** These are the usual reasons.

- Wrong category. A software service listed under an unrelated category will not appear when a buyer searches or raises a bid.
- Vague scope. "Software development" with one price tells the buyer nothing. State what is included: modules, users, hosting, training, support.
- Stale information. An expired certificate, an old address or an unanswered query makes the vendor look closed.
- Ignoring eligibility terms. Bids often carry conditions on past experience or turnover. Read them before quoting.

The fix is one person in the company who owns the GeM account, checks it as part of regular work and does not miss bids raised in its category.

## Common questions

### Can a department buy custom software on GeM, or only ready products?

Yes, custom software can generally be bought on GeM, because the portal covers services as well as goods. Check which service category fits the work, and ask the GeM helpdesk or your accounts office if none does.

### Does seeing a demo bind the department to that vendor?

No, a demo creates no obligation on either side. The purchase still follows the GeM mode that applies to its value, and other eligible sellers can compete where that mode requires it.

### Does Udyam registration guarantee orders on GeM?

No, Udyam registration makes a vendor eligible for certain benefits but does not bring orders by itself. Orders come from a correct listing, a clear scope and active participation in bids. Confirm the current benefits with the department or your CA.
