A hospital owner in Jalgaon sits through a software demo on a Saturday afternoon. The salesperson clicks through a clean dashboard, every screen works, and a quote arrives on WhatsApp the same evening. The next vendor shows a different dashboard that looks just as good.

The owner now has two quotes and no way to compare them. Demos are built to look alike. The real differences sit in the answers to questions nobody asked.

## Why questions work better than feature lists

Every brochure lists OPD, pharmacy, lab, billing and IPD. That tells you what the software has. It does not tell you whether your receptionist will use it on a crowded Monday, what happens to your records if you leave, or who answers when billing stops during evening OPD.

The ten questions below are grouped by what they protect: your daily work, your data and your money. Put **the same ten questions to every vendor** and write the answers side by side.

## Questions about daily use

1. Does every module work on a phone? A small hospital in Latur or Amravati typically has one or two computers and a phone in every pocket. If the doctor cannot write a prescription on a phone and the owner cannot check the day's collection on one, the software stays at the billing desk.

2. Does it work in Marathi? Check the staff screens, the patient's bill and the messages sent to patients, not only the login page. A pharmacy assistant who has to guess at English menus will go back to the register.

3. Can I decide what each staff member sees? The receptionist needs tokens. The pharmacist needs stock. Only the owner should see total collections or change rates. Ask whether you set these roles from your own login, or must call the vendor every time someone joins or leaves.

## Questions about your data

4. Who owns the data, and can I export it? The answer should be that the hospital owns it, **stated in writing**. Ask to see the export itself: patients, bills and stock in a file that opens in a spreadsheet. If you change vendors one day, that file is what you carry out.

5. Where is the data hosted? Patient records are sensitive, and data protection law in India places duties on whoever holds them. Ask whether the servers are in India and who can access them. Confirm what the law requires of your hospital with a legal advisor.

6. How are backups taken, and has a restore been tested? Ask how often backups run, where the copies are kept, and whether the vendor has actually restored data from one. A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a backup.

## Questions about money and effort

7. What is the full price, and is there a large setup fee? Ask for one figure that covers the subscription, setup, per-user charges, message charges and yearly maintenance. Software for a small hospital should not need a setup fee that dwarfs the subscription. We have written separately about [what hospital software should cost a 30-bed hospital](/blog/small-hospital-software-cost/).

8. What does setup need from my side? Ask for the steps in order: who creates the logins, who enters the doctors, the rate list, the medicine stock and the lab tests, and which department starts first. If the plan needs new computers or a visiting engineer, count that cost too.

9. Who trains the staff, and in which language? Staff changes are normal in a small hospital. Ask whether training is repeated for new joiners, whether it is in Marathi, and whether there are short guides the night shift can open on a phone.

10. Who do I call when something stops? A hospital does not close at six. Ask for the support number, the hours, the language, and whether support is included in the price or billed per call.

## Red flags in vendor answers

Listen to how each question is answered, not only to what is said.

- "We will discuss export later." If export is not shown in the demo, assume it does not exist.
- A price that keeps changing, or a setup fee with no list of what it pays for.
- "It works on mobile", but the demo only ever runs on a laptop.
- One shared username and password for the whole staff.
- "It is all in the cloud" as the full answer on backups.
- Pressure to sign today for a special discount.

One vague answer is not a reason to reject a vendor. Several in a row show you how things will go after you have paid.

## Ask for a demo with your own doctors and departments

A demo with sample data proves the software works for an imaginary hospital. Ask for one set up with **your own doctors and departments**, a few of your real rates and some medicines from your pharmacy.

Then let your staff run it, not the salesperson. The receptionist issues a token. The doctor writes a prescription on a phone. The pharmacist bills it. The owner opens the day's collection report. Watch where each person gets stuck.

A vendor who is confident in the product will agree to this before any payment. A vendor who refuses has answered an eleventh question for you.

## Common questions

### Should a small hospital choose cloud software or installed software?

Either can work, so decide on the answers to questions 4, 5 and 6 rather than on the label. Cloud software is usually backed up by the vendor, while an installed system keeps data inside the building but leaves backups and repairs to you.

### Is free hospital software worth trying?

It can be, if the answers on data ownership and support are still clear. Free software often earns its money elsewhere, through paid support, paid export or limits on patient numbers, so ask what the paid parts are before you enter real records.

### Can old records be moved into new software?

Usually some of them can, and you should ask each vendor exactly which ones. Patient lists, rate lists and medicine stock kept in a spreadsheet are typically simple to import, while handwritten registers may need manual entry. Ask who does the import and whether it is included in the price.
