A party launches its app at a district melava in Solapur. The link goes into every WhatsApp group, booth pramukhs install it in front of the taluka adhyaksha, and screenshots are posted as proof. By the next review meeting, reports are coming in the old way again: photos in WhatsApp groups, late phone calls, and a diary in someone's shirt pocket.

The karyakarta is not being lazy. The app asked for more than it gave back. If you head a party organisation or manage a campaign, here is why that happens, and what an app must do so that the person standing at the booth opens it every day.

## Why most party apps are abandoned

Most party apps are designed from the top down. The state office lists everything it wants to know, a vendor turns each wish into a screen, and the result lands on the phone of a farmer in Jalgaon or an auto driver in Nashik who has other work to do.

Three things typically finish it off:

- Too many screens: the home page carries news, speeches, a photo gallery, membership, events and surveys. The one thing the worker came to do is buried under all of it.
- English first: menus say "Dashboard", "Submit" and "Sync". A worker who reads Marathi comfortably has to guess, and after two wrong guesses stops trying.
- Heavy downloads: the app is large, the phone is already full of family photos and WhatsApp videos, and each update asks for space that is not there. The app is deleted to make room.

Leaders then conclude that karyakartas dislike technology. The same karyakartas use WhatsApp and UPI without any training.

## What a ground worker opens every day

Ask a booth worker what they need from the party on their phone and the list is short.

1. My tasks: what am I supposed to do today? Visit these houses, bring these people to the sabha, confirm the booth committee names. A short list with a tick against each item.
2. My booth: the households, the committee members and the pending work for their own booth, and nothing about anyone else's.
3. A way to report: one button to say the work is done, with a photo or a voice note if needed. No form with twelve fields.

**If the first screen shows only these three things, the app has a reason to be opened.** Whatever else the head office wants, such as a survey or event attendance, should arrive as a task in that list, not as a new menu.

## Marathi first, on the phone they already own

Marathi first does not mean an English app run through translation. It means labels in the words the organisation already uses: booth pramukh, mandal, baithak, sampark. English stays available for those who want it.

The phone matters as much as the language. Plan for a low-cost Android handset with little free storage and a network that drops between the field and the town. That means:

- a small download, with updates that do not demand fresh storage each time
- a task list that opens without network and holds reports on the phone until the signal returns
- photos compressed before sending, so one report does not eat the day's data
- large buttons and voice notes for workers who prefer speaking to typing

This is the standard for our [field and citizen apps](/services/mobile-apps/): they must work on cheap phones and weak networks.

## Recognition keeps people reporting

Karyakartas are not paid staff. They work out of belief, belonging and the hope of being noticed. An app that only collects data feels like supervision. An app that shows their work to the people above them feels like recognition.

A leader board ranks booths or mandals on completed work. Keep it fair by comparing like with like: booth against booth inside a mandal, not a city ward in Pune against a rural booth in Amravati. Count verified work, not taps.

**Acknowledgement from leaders** matters more than rank. When the district adhyaksha or the candidate can see the best booths of the week and send a named message of thanks from [the dashboard](/blog/booth-committee-dashboard/), the worker shows it at home and at the chowk. That message is what brings them back to the app.

## Keep the karyakarta's own data private

Workers hesitate over party apps for one more reason, which they rarely say aloud: what is this app taking from my phone, and who can see me?

Answer it in the design.

- **Ask only for what the work needs.** A reporting app has no reason to read a worker's contact list or gallery.
- Record location only at the moment a report is filed, if at all, and say so on the screen in Marathi.
- Show a worker's phone number only to office bearers in their own line, by role, and keep one mandal's list closed to another.

A party also holds data about voters and citizens, which carries duties under election and [data protection rules](/blog/dpdp-act-government-software/). Our approach is on the [election technology](/services/election-technology/) page. Confirm what applies to you with your legal advisor.

## Common questions

### Will older karyakartas use an app at all?

Yes, if it opens in Marathi and the first screen shows only their own work. Many senior workers already use WhatsApp, so the phone is not the barrier. Too many menus are.

### Does the app work where the network is poor?

It should, and you should ask the vendor to prove it with mobile data switched off. A well-built karyakarta app opens the task list without network, saves the report on the phone and sends it when the signal returns.

### Who can see a karyakarta's phone number and location?

Only the people whose role requires it, such as the worker's own mandal and district office bearers. Access should be role-based and decided by the organisation, not by the vendor.
