At a dental clinic in Nashik, the receptionist has tomorrow's appointment list and one phone. Between billing and the patients standing at her desk, she calls each number to confirm. Many do not pick up. By evening the list is half done and nobody knows which slots are really filled.

A coaching institute in Latur has the same problem with a different list: enquiries from last week's advertisement that nobody has called back. The calls are simple. There are just too many of them, and they always lose to the work in front of the desk.

## The calls your staff make every day

Listen to any front desk for a day and most outgoing calls fall into three kinds.

- Reminders: "Your appointment is tomorrow at 11." "Your instalment is due this week."
- Confirmations: "Are you coming tomorrow?" The answer changes what the office does next.
- First follow-up: "You enquired about our course. Which batch are you asking about?"

These calls have a fixed script and a short answer. They are also the first to be skipped on a busy day. A missed reminder typically means an empty slot, the problem behind [follow-up reminders that bring patients back](/blog/patient-follow-up-reminders/). A late first call typically means the enquiry has already spoken to someone else.

## Where an AI voice call helps

An AI voice call is a phone call where a computer voice speaks a script, listens to the reply and records the outcome. It suits a call when the script is short, the answers are few and a misheard answer does little harm. Good fits:

- Appointment reminders where the person can confirm, cancel or ask for another time.
- Fee, renewal or service reminders that state one fact and offer a callback.
- The first call to a new enquiry: confirm they enquired, ask one or two sorting questions, and fix a time for a staff member to call.

The machine does not talk better than your receptionist. The gain is that **every number on the list gets called**, and each result is written against the name: confirmed, cancelled, wants a callback, no answer. For lead lists, the same record shows which sources send [leads that never answer the phone](/blog/lead-quality-stop-paying-for-bad-leads/).

Voice is not always the first tool. Many reminders work as a message through [WhatsApp automation](/services/whatsapp-automation/), with a call kept for people who do not reply or prefer to listen.

## Where a person must call

Some calls should never go to a machine.

- Anything medical beyond date, time and place: test results, medicine doubts, symptoms.
- Complaints, money disputes, refunds and overdue payments where the person is already upset.
- Fee negotiation, counselling a parent, closing a sale.

The system also needs a handover rule. If the person says something outside the script, sounds confused or asks for a human, the call should say that a staff member will call back and create a **callback task** with what was said. It should not improvise an answer.

## Marathi and Hindi voice

A reminder in English to a family in Parbhani or Jalgaon is likely to be cut short. The call should be in the language the person speaks at home.

- Save a language preference against each contact. If it is unknown, open in Marathi and offer Hindi.
- Write the script in spoken Marathi, not the Marathi of printed forms. Keep the English words people really use, such as appointment, report and batch.
- Test how the voice says names, town names, amounts and times.
- Keep the expected answers simple: a clear "ho" or "nahi", with a keypad press as a fallback.

Before any customer hears the voice, **have your own staff take test calls** and correct what sounds wrong.

## Say it is an automated call

Tell people at the start that they are speaking to an automated system. Do not give the voice a human name. People tend to speak more plainly once they know, and an office that hides it loses trust when someone finds out.

A good call follows this order:

1. Who is calling, and that the call is automated.
2. Why, in one sentence.
3. The question, with the choices.
4. A repeat of what was understood.
5. How to reach a person, and how to stop these calls.

## Consent, calling hours and do-not-call preferences

Consent: call only people who gave you their number for this purpose, such as a patient who booked or a parent who filled an enquiry form. Do not load purchased lists. Keep a record of how consent was given. Clinics should also read [what the DPDP Act changes for patient data](/blog/clinic-patient-data-dpdp-act/).

Calling hours: India has telecom rules on commercial calls, including do-not-disturb preferences that subscribers register. Confirm the current rules with your telecom provider and a legal advisor before the first call. Then set a calling window in the system so it cannot dial outside it.

Do-not-call: when someone says "do not call me", save it against the number. **Every future list must respect that opt-out**, including lists your staff dial by hand.

## Common questions

### Can AI voice calls replace our receptionist or telecaller?

No. They take over fixed-script calls so staff have time for calls that need judgement, like a worried patient or a parent comparing fees. A person still approves the script and the list.

### Will people accept an automated call in Marathi?

It depends on the call, so test with a small list of your own contacts first. Typically, a short call in their own language from a name they know is heard out, and a long or pushy one is cut.

### Do we need permission before making automated reminder calls?

Treat consent as necessary: call only people who shared their number with you for that purpose. Telecom and data protection rules both apply, so confirm the details with your telecom provider and a legal advisor.
